The Imprinted Sphere

Sand on a vibrating plate doesn't copy the sound — it collects along the lines where the plate isn't moving. Change the frequency and the plate picks a different standing pattern. This model does the same thing on a sphere, then asks the hard question: if the shell were built from cells that latch when pushed hard enough, would the pattern still be there after the sound stopped?

Why the material is the interesting part

In an ordinary solid the wave speed is fixed, so frequency and wavelength trade off in a straight line. Build the shell out of tiny mass-on-spring cells and that line bends: near the cells' own resonance the wave crawls, wavelengths collapse, and over one band the wave cannot travel through the material at all.

The chain, link by link

Four of these five links are ordinary physics that a bench could confirm this month. The fifth is the whole idea, and it is the one nobody here has evidence for.

The cheapest way to find out you're wrong

A sphere is the expensive version of this experiment. A flat plate answers the same question for the price of a shaker and an afternoon — and if the answer there is no, the sphere never needs building.

STAGE 01

Does it pattern?

Print a flat lattice with the cell stiffness varied across it, sweep the frequency, film the surface. You are checking one thing: does each frequency give a repeatable, distinct pattern? Cost: low. Kills the idea fastest.

STAGE 02

Does it stay?

Swap in cells that snap between two stable shapes. Drive hard enough that the loudest regions snap over. Cut the power. The measurement is simply: what fraction of the cells that should have flipped are still flipped an hour later?

STAGE 03

Does it stay right?

Compare the frozen pattern against the live one you filmed in Stage 01. Snapping cells push on their neighbours, so the frozen shape can drift away from the mode that made it. That error, in a number, is the real result.

What this model is and isn't

Honest about

  • The mode shapes are really computed — associated Legendre functions on the sphere, not a decorative texture.
  • The dispersion curve is the standard mass-in-mass result: effective mass goes negative just above the cells' resonance, which is exactly what opens the stop band. (That negative-mass reading traces to Liu, Chan & Sheng 2005 — not to the 2000 paper it is usually credited to.)
  • Patterns fade between resonances because a real shell only rings at discrete frequencies — that's the "coupling" number dropping.
  • The validity limit is shown, not hidden: once λ falls below two cell widths, the smooth-material story is no longer true.

Assumed, and load-bearing

  • Drive direction is simplified to picking one pattern family. A real shaker excites a blend, which would smear the picture.
  • Losses are ignored. Damping decides whether the drive can ever reach the latch threshold — if it can't, none of this works.
  • Every cell is assumed to latch at the same threshold. Manufacturing spread is the most likely thing to destroy the pattern.
  • The reverse problem — pick a shape, get the frequency that makes it — is not solved here or anywhere in this repo.
Nothing on this page has been measured. The numbers come from a small analytic model running in your browser — a 120 mm shell, a 120 m/s baseline wave speed, cells resonating at 1200 Hz with a 0.6 mass ratio and a 12 mm pitch — chosen to be plausible, not because a part exists. Classical sources are named from memory and were not fetched in this pass. Adjacent work in this repository (bias-tuned reflecting surfaces) is electromagnetic rather than acoustic; the shared idea is that the structure, not the bulk material, decides how a wave behaves.
