Does a lossy resonant cell fit inside a 2 mm printed skin?

Issue #128 argues the absorber's dissipation belongs in the printed pattern, not in the substrate — because a pattern is geometry, and geometry is what the design loop can search. This bench draws the resulting cell to scale at 10 GHz and checks it against four hard limits: the Voltera NOVA's 0.2 mm feature floor, the patent's 0.87–2.0 mm thickness budget, the sheet resistance the available inks can actually reach, and whether the surface absorbs 90% of the power that hits it. Every dimension below is a real millimetre. Drive it and see which cells survive.

What this model is, and what it is not

It is a circuit model, not a solve. The printed layer is treated as one lumped resistance in series with one capacitance; the substrate-plus-mirror underneath is a shorted transmission line. Everything is CALCULATED from that. It gets resonant frequency and match right to within the tens of percent, which is all a fit question needs. It does not capture the coupling between neighbouring cells, and it does not describe a two-resonator cell (design C) properly at all — the caveat is flagged on that design.

Sheet resistance — ohms per square — is the one number that decides whether a printed film absorbs or reflects. It is the film's resistivity divided by its thickness, and it does not depend on how big the square is, which is why it is quoted "per square". Free space is 377 Ω/sq; a film near that value is the classic radar-absorbing sheet.

Squares in the current path is the idea this prototype turns on. A resistive film's contribution to the cell is not its sheet resistance — it is its sheet resistance multiplied by how many square tiles long the current path is. A thin ring 10 squares long multiplies the ink's resistance tenfold; a short wide bridge divides it. That factor is pure geometry, and it is the knob the design loop can search. Computed here from the uniform-current approximation, so treat the factor as order-of-magnitude, not three significant figures.

Skin depth is how far a radio wave gets into a conductor before fading to about a third. At 10 GHz it is 3.4 µm in printed silver and 390 µm in printed carbon — which is why a 10 µm silver film is already a perfect mirror, and a 10 µm carbon film is essentially transparent and behaves like a pure resistor.

Grid capacitance follows Luukkonen's standard analytical patch-array form, with the effective permittivity taken as the average of air and substrate. Free-space impedance 377 Ω, wavelength 29.979 mm at 10 GHz. Ink figures come from the ACI SC1502 and SS1109 datasheets and from docs/voltera-multilayer-capability.md; substrate figures from docs/xband-absorber-substrate-shortlist.md; the I-shape dimensions from Landy et al. 2008 via docs/ishape-interior-tuning.md.

The bandpass layer is a square-loop slot array — a metal sheet with resonant slots cut in it — modelled through Langley and Parker's equivalent circuit for the complementary metal loop array, flipped by Babinet's principle. The higher-order correction term is dropped, which most likely makes the predicted passband wider than the real one. So read the bandwidth as indicative, and treat the rasorber's failure (design E) as provisional rather than settled — a sharper filter than this model predicts would improve it.

The printed insulator cannot separate two patterned layers. A patch layer over a wire mesh at 30 µm overlaps by roughly 73 mm² per cell, which at εr 3 is about 64 pF against a 0.38 pF design value — a hundred and seventy times over. The layers are capacitively shorted together. That is why the bandpass here is cut from a single layer instead: the substrate is the only real spacer this process has, for a filter exactly as much as for an absorber.

Normal incidence only. No oblique-angle behaviour, no polarisation sweep, no bending. Those are real questions and this bench does not touch them.
